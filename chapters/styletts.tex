This chapter introduces neural \acp{CDE} experiments applied to speech synthesis. This presents a new challenge as each sample from the continuous hidden state must be optimised, unlike simpler tasks such as speech emotion recognition where only the final state is optimised directly \citep{ni2025speechemotioncde}.
\ainote{Describe supervision at multiple output times rather than optimisation of hidden-state samples themselves: the model parameters are optimised through the synthesis loss. Scope the final-state comparison to the cited speech-emotion model.}

As briefly discussed in the previous chapter, \acp{CDE} are a promising tool for speech synthesis as they produce a hidden state that interacts with an irregularly sampled control path. An important stage in speech synthesis is expanding the hidden state from the number of phone labels to the number of acoustic frames. The mapping between the two is monotonic and irregularly aligned. Different phones have different durations due to physiological and physical factors and speaking style. This is the core motivation for investigating \acp{CDE} as they share this irregular mapping property.

In detail, the method lies between a text encoder and an acoustic decoder. The goal of the neural \ac{CDE} is to produce time-informed features for the decoder. Such features vary with the predicted alignment of the utterance; without a \ac{CDE}, the phone features would not vary based on the time evolution of the signal, introducing a lack of variance.

The models considered in this work resolve speech-text alignment by repeating each text feature for its predicted duration. A phone's duration can have a significant effect on how it sounds. For example, an exceptionally long vowel may become tired over time, and a short phone may be a short burst of energy or omitted entirely. Simply repeating a text feature misses the opportunity to encode such nuanced properties into the model.

The integration of \acp{CDE} is evaluated in two experimental setups. The first uses read speech; the second examines the time-aware properties of the neural \ac{CDE} in greater detail through emotional speech synthesis guided by a style reference.

The emotional speech synthesis task addressed here involves a text prompt and a speech prompt. The goal is to narrate the text with the same speaker identity and emotional style as the speech prompt.

\section{Emotional speech synthesis guided by reference style}
The core challenge addressed in this chapter involves synthesising speech in the same style as a reference sample, with attention to emotional intensity. The system uses an encoder to produce a control path for a \ac{CDE}, whose hidden-state trajectory is then sampled and mapped to a waveform by a decoder. The non-CDE modules are initialised by training StyleTTS 2 on LibriTTS (multi-speaker audiobook narration data, neutral emotion) \citep{li2023styletts2}. StyleTTS 2 introduces \ac{SLM} training, where an adversarial objective is used with WavLM features \citep{chen2022wavlm}, an effective yet computationally demanding process. Memory use is reduced through mixed precision, maximum acoustic lengths of 175 or 800 frames, a batch size of 6 without \ac{SLM} adversarial training, and a batch size of 4 with \ac{SLM} training. The model is trained for a total of 5 epochs, with style diffusion being trained from the second epoch and \ac{SLM} from the fourth. All modules are trainable. This approach allows fine-tuning on an NVIDIA A100/H100 GPU with less than 70 GB of video memory. StyleTTS 2 checkpoints before and after fine-tuning serve as baseline models, denoted as StyleTTS 2 (LibriTTS) and StyleTTS 2 (\ac{ESD}), respectively.

The \ac{LSTM} baseline is a separate system using a recurrent module with duration-augmented inputs. This setup uses an ASR-based text aligner. F5-TTS is included as a baseline independent of StyleTTS, based on its reported high-quality zero-shot synthesis \citep{chen-etal-2024-f5tts}. Studies with autoregressive models are left as future work. Neural \acp{CDE} can also be interpreted as continuous-time recurrent models \citep{kidger2020neuralcde}. To examine whether the observed behaviour arises from sequential capacity rather than continuous dynamics, an experiment compares several \ac{CDE} configurations with an \ac{LSTM}. Both models are trained with the \textit{duration-augmented control path} $\rmX$ provided as input.

\section{Emotional speech synthesis implementation details}
First, the initial hidden state $\rvz_0$ is predicted with an \ac{LSTM} that reads the control sequence in reverse and compresses its channels. This encodes the full control context in the initial state, with the first phone $\rvx_0$ observed last: $\rvz_0\coloneqq \textnormal{LSTM}(\rmX_{L,\dots,0})$. To reduce variation in the control variable $\rmX$, the time channel is normalised by the total phone duration for the utterance.
\ainote{Define $L$ and distinguish phone indices, acoustic-frame indices and continuous time. State whether total duration is supplied separately: normalising the time channel removes its absolute scale. Specify the control interpolation and the units of the RK4 step size $h$ reported in the results.}

Many \ac{CDE} implementations \citep{kidger2020neuralcde} parametrise the vector field $\rmF_\vtheta$ with a multilayer perceptron whose final output has dimension $dC$, reshaped into the matrix required by the \ac{CDE} equation (Equation~\ref{eq:ode_ct}). With 512 control channels and hidden dimension 128, this final layer would output 65,536 values from 128 hidden units. This can be computationally expensive. The approach proposed here uses a U-Net \citep{ronneberger2015unet} with one downsampling stage, 256 middle channels, and 512 upsampling channels. It strides along the hidden dimension $d$ and produces the matrix needed to evaluate the \ac{CDE} (Equation~\ref{eq:ode_ct}). The \ac{CDE} is integrated with the fixed-step fourth-order Runge--Kutta method \citep{Runge1895UeberDN,kutta1901beitrag}. The sampled hidden sequence $\rmZ_{0,\dots,L}$ is then upsampled to 512 channels with another U-Net that processes each sampled state. A gated residual connection, $\rmH^\textnormal{cde}\coloneqq \rmH^\textnormal{txt}+\gamma\rmZ$, uses a trainable parameter $\gamma$ to integrate the \ac{CDE} module with the pretrained model during fine-tuning. \ainote{Define $C$ consistently with the earlier $c+1$ channels, including time: confirm whether 512 includes the time channel. Distinguish the 128-dimensional hidden sequence from its 512-channel projection in the residual equation, and specify the gate shape and initial value.}

The Adam optimiser's $\beta_1$ is set to 0.9 for the \ac{CDE} and to 0 elsewhere. The remaining setup follows the Matcha-TTS/StyleTTS 2 fine-tuning configuration \citep{mehta2024matcha,li2023styletts2}. The CDE-processed phone-level representations are then upsampled for the decoder. Although the reparameterisation-invariance theorem \citep{kidger2021thesis} suggests that phone-level operation can provide a sufficiently expressive model, this chapter leaves open whether operating at one frame per step gives a richer temporal progression at the cost of more \ac{ODE} solver steps.

\section{Evaluation}
The subjective quality of the models is evaluated with emotion intensity mean opinion score (MOS-EI) through a \ac{MUSHRA}-type setup on the MTurk crowdsourcing platform.\footnote{\url{https://www.mturk.com/}, permanently closed on September 30, 2026.} A total of 25 participants were asked to listen to a reference recording, followed by synthetic and ground-truth recordings presented in a random order. The listener is only aware of which sample belongs to the reference split. The reference is given to the \ac{TTS} model to condition style, but not linguistic content. The participant then rates each clip on a 1--5 integer scale for how strongly the emotion is expressed in the audio, e.g. ``how happy is this speech?''. This scheme is inspired by the valence-arousal model of emotion \citep{POSNER_RUSSELL_PETERSON_2005}, where the label, e.g. angry, gives a hint to the target valence-arousal, and the user judges a subjective level of arousal/intensity. The mean opinion of this test reveals how strongly different systems express a target emotion, and the Spearman rank correlation of subjective scores with the reference measures reference-style tracking, while the \ac{MAE} measures reference-style calibration. Overall, each sample was listened to by an average of 10 listeners.

Although \ac{MUSHRA} is effective at evaluating many systems, listeners pay less attention to subtle effects, motivating comparative MOS (CMOS) \citep{li2023styletts2,ju2024naturalspeech3,minixhofer2024ttsds}. CMOS is an A/B test that presents two samples at a time and prompts the user to rate which one is better. This evaluation uses emotion-expression quality CMOS (CMOS-EQ) with an integer scale from $-3$ to $+3$ to represent how much better the target emotion is expressed in system B than in system A, with negative scores meaning system B is worse than system A. One of the systems will always be the candidate, and the other one of the designated baselines, displayed in random order. In both \ac{MOS} tests, the listeners are prompted to focus on emotional content. Consistency was checked by duplicating 10 comparisons and swapping the A/B order without informing participants; all participants gave consistent responses for at least 5 comparisons.
\ainote{Define consistency for swapped A/B trials, including ties, and report participant screening and exclusions. Explain score-sign correction after randomisation, the number of comparisons per system pair, and uncertainty estimates. Support or qualify the claim that multi-stimulus tests reduce attention to subtle effects.}

A question arising from these studies concerns how listeners perceive unlabelled emotional speech. This is answered by measuring emotion accuracy, which prompts the user with a simple data annotation task given both real and synthetic recordings to identify how well intended emotions are expressed by different systems, and how they are perceived. Each sample is classified by 20 participants.

Objective metrics such as \ac{MCD} and log-$F_0$ \ac{RMSE} are used to measure how different design choices affect model outputs. \ac{MCD} measures the difference between the mel-cepstra of generated and ground-truth speech, with lower values correlating with audio quality/similarity. log-$F_0$ \ac{RMSE} measures the error of the pitch contour of the generated signal; lower values correlate with correct intonation, emotion and prosody.

Table~\ref{tab:emotion_combined} reports the listening-test results. Panel (a) gives the Spearman rank correlation between system and reference ratings. The macro correlation is computed by Fisher $z$-transforming the per-emotion correlations, averaging in $z$-space, and transforming back. Panel (b) reports the \ac{MAE} from reference ratings, while panel (c) reports mean emotion intensity. Higher intensity alone does not imply better synthesis: the tracking and calibration measures assess different aspects of matching the reference ratings.

No synthetic system leads across all emotions and metrics. All synthetic systems have lower mean intensity ratings than the reference in every emotion category. StyleTTS 2 (\ac{ESD}) has positive correlations for neutral, happy and surprised speech, and negative correlations for anger and sadness. Among the baseline systems, only F5-TTS for neutral speech has a correlation marked as statistically significant in panel (a).

Relative to StyleTTS 2 (\ac{ESD}), the single-layer \ac{CDE} with $h=1.0$ has higher correlations for anger, happiness and sadness, but lower correlations for neutral and surprised speech. Its macro correlation is 0.53, compared with 0.08 for that baseline, and its macro \ac{MAE} is 0.341, compared with 0.373. \section{Summary}\label{sec:conclusion}
This chapter introduced neural \acp{CDE} as a continuous-time mechanism for duration-aware acoustic modelling in text-to-speech. The proposed method augments phone-level representations with duration-derived timing information and uses a neural acoustic vector field to produce hidden states whose values evolve with phonetic content and temporal structure, rather than using duration only for length regulation. Experiments show that \acp{CDE} provide a small benefit on neutral audiobook-style speech, but can improve reference-style tracking in emotional \ac{TTS}. In particular, a single-layer \ac{CDE} with $h=1.0$ achieved the strongest macro Spearman correlation with reference emotion-intensity ratings, while CMOS-EQ results indicated comparable utterance-level emotion-expression quality and a modest listener-level preference over the fine-tuned StyleTTS 2 baseline. The results also show that step size and layer depth affect the trade-off between style tracking, calibration, and perceived emotion-expression quality, suggesting that temporal resolution is a meaningful modelling choice in CDE-based \ac{TTS}. These findings motivate future investigation into alternative interpolation schemes, solver choices, frame-level control paths, and larger-scale evaluation across more diverse speakers, styles, and data resources.
