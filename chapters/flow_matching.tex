The original SB problem describes paths between two observations at $u=0$ and $u=1$. The paths are required to have specified endpoint marginals and are compared with a reference path law $P_\mathrm{ref}$. The Schr\"odinger bridge minimises path-space Kullback--Leibler divergence subject to those marginal constraints:
\[
\min _{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\left[0,1\right]}(p_0,p_1)} D_{\text{KL}}(P\Vert P_\mathrm{ref})
\]
Under standard regularity conditions, the solution can be represented using positive Schr\"odinger potentials $\Psi_u$ and $\hat\Psi_u$ in the forward and reverse SDEs
\[
\dd\rmX \coloneqq \left[\rmF(\rmX,u) + g^2(u)\nabla\log\mPsi_u\right]\dd u + g(u)\dd \rmW_u
\]
\[
\dd\rmX \coloneqq \left[\rmF(\rmX,u) - g^2(u)\nabla\log\hat\mPsi_u\right]\dd u + g(u)\dd \hat\rmW_u
\]
The time-$u$ marginal density is proportional to the product $\hat\Psi_u\Psi_u$; the proportionality constant is fixed by normalisation.
Assume linear drift $\rmF(\rmX)=f(u)\rmX$ and Gaussian boundaries
\[
p_0=\gN(\rmX,\epsilon_0^2\tI) \quad p_1=\gN(\rmY,\epsilon_1^2\tI)
\]
where $\epsilon_1=e^{\int_0^1f(u)\dd u}\epsilon_0$. As $\epsilon_0\rightarrow 0$, the closed-form solution \citep{bunne2023schrodinger} is expressed as 
\[
\hat\mPsi_u=\gN\left(e^{\int_0^uf(s)\dd s}\rmX,e^{2\int_0^uf(s)\dd s}\int_0^u\frac{g^2(s)}{e^{2\int_0^sf(v)\dd v}}\dd s\tI\right)
\]
\[
\mPsi_u=\gN\left(\frac{e^{\int_0^uf(s)\dd s}}{e^{\int_0^1f(s)\dd s}}\rmY,e^{2\int_0^uf(s)\dd s}\left[\int_0^1\frac{g^2(s)}{e^{2\int_0^sf(v)\dd v}}\dd s - \int_0^u\frac{g^2(s)}{e^{2\int_0^sf(v)\dd v}}\dd s\right]\tI\right)
\]
Given that the forward $\mPsi$ is guaranteed to have mean $\rmY$ at $u=1$, and the reverse is guaranteed to have mean $\rmX$ at $u=0$, the solution can be simplified by considering reference paths with zero drift $f(u)=0$, leading to
\[
\hat\mPsi_u=\gN\left(\rmX,\int_0^ug^2(s)\dd s\tI\right)
\]
\[
\mPsi_u=\gN\left(\rmY,\int_0^1g^2(s)\dd s - \int_0^ug^2(s)\dd s\tI\right)
\]
the mean and covariance of the solution $p$ are
\[
\rmM(\rmX,\rmY,u)=\left(1-\frac{\int_0^ug^2(s)\dd s}{\int_0^1g^2(s)\dd s}\right)\rmX + \left(\frac{\int_0^ug^2(s)\dd s}{\int_0^1g^2(s)\dd s}\right)\rmY
\]
\[
\tSigma = \int_0^ug^2(s)\dd s\left(1-\frac{\int_0^ug^2(s)\dd s}{\int_0^1g^2(s)\dd s}\right)\tI
\]
This solution is related to the diffusion framework described in \chapref{chap:diffusion}. Bridge paths can connect prescribed endpoint distributions, and this Gaussian example has zero conditional variance at its boundaries. This property alone does not establish lower prediction error or better generalisation than a mean-reverting SDE.

An important distinction with supervised learning is that samples from the boundaries are paired
\[
(\rmX^{(i)},\rmY^{(i)})\sim\pi(\rmX,\rmY)
\]

\section{Example: variance-exploding SB}
Consider a purely variance-exploding reference process
\[
\dd \rmX \coloneqq \bm 0 \dd u + \sqrt{ck^{2u}}\dd\rmW_u
\]
\[
\sigma_u^2 \coloneqq \frac{c(k^{2u}-1)}{2 \log k},
\]
\[
  \label{eq:sb_mean_variance}
  \rmM_u(\rmX_0, \rmY) = \left(1 - \frac{\sigma_u^2}{\sigma_1^2}\right) \rmX_0 + \frac{\sigma_u^2}{\sigma_1^2} \rmY ,
\]
\[
\tSigma(u) = \sigma_u^2 \left(1 - \frac{\sigma_u^2}{\sigma_1^2}\right)\tI,
\]

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{plots/k_c_plot-11.pdf}
    \caption{The choice of base $k$ and scale $c$ affects interpolation weight $\frac{\sigma_u^2}{\sigma_1^2}$ and variance $\tSigma$. Interpolation becomes linear as $k\rightarrow1$. Variance is always $0$ at the boundaries, and scales with $c$.}
    \label{fig:sb_kc}
\end{figure}

For this example, a data-prediction objective regresses the clean endpoint from a path sample:
\[
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DP}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_{(\rmX_0,\rmY)\sim\pi,\,\ru\sim U(0,1),\,\rmX_\ru\sim p_{\ru|0,Y}(\cdot|\rmX_0,\rmY)}\left[\left\|\mS_\theta(\rmX_\ru\mid\mY,\ru)-\rmX_0\right\|^2\right]
\]

\section{Notes on the SB design space}\label{sec:sbsv}
As seen in \figref{fig:sb_kc}, the mean $\rmM$ tends to linear interpolation as $k\rightarrow1$. The variance $\tSigma$ is $0$ at the boundaries and increases towards the centre. This dynamic movement can make deep learning more difficult. Given that this variance is artificial, it is important to determine the benefits of this noise.

A simpler conditional path uses a linearly interpolating mean and constant covariance.
\[
\dd \rmX_u= (\mY-\rmX_u)\dd u
\]
The conditional mean follows a straight path, with $\frac{\dd}{\dd u}\rmM_u=\mY-\rmX_0$. The target $\rmX_0-\mY$ below is therefore the velocity in the reverse depth direction (from $u=1$ towards $u=0$), which is the convention used for direct prediction. This is McCann's displacement interpolation only in the zero-noise limit and when the endpoint coupling is optimal for the transport cost. The conditional path is described as
\[
\rmM(\rmX_0,\rmY,u) = u\rmY + (1-u)\rmX_0 \quad \tSigma(u)= c\tI
\label{eq:icfm_mean_variance}
\]
\[
\vtheta^\ast=\arg\min_\vtheta\mathbb E_{(\rmX_0,\rmY)\sim\pi,\,\ru\sim U(0,1),\,\rmZ\sim\mathcal N(0,I)}\left[\left\|\rmF_\vtheta(\rmX_\ru\mid\mY,\ru)-(\rmX_0 -\mY)\right\|^2\right],\quad \rmX_\ru=\rmM(\rmX_0,\rmY,\ru)+\sqrt c\rmZ
\]

\section{Inference schemes}
The ODE sampler that solves the SB-VE problem is defined as
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mX_{u_{i-1}}
={}&
\frac{
    \sigma_{u_{i-1}}\sqrt{\sigma_1^2-\sigma_{u_{i-1}}^2}
}{
    \sigma_{u_i}\sqrt{\sigma_1^2-\sigma_{u_i}^2}
}
\mX_{u_i}
\\
&+
\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}
\left(
    \sigma_1^2-\sigma_{u_{i-1}}^2
    -
    \frac{
        \sqrt{\sigma_1^2-\sigma_{u_i}^2}
        \sigma_{u_{i-1}}
        \sqrt{\sigma_1^2-\sigma_{u_{i-1}}^2}
    }{
        \sigma_{u_i}
    }
\right)
    \mD_\vtheta(\mX_{u_i},\mY,u_i)
\\
&+
\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}
\left(
    \sigma_{u_{i-1}}^2
    -
    \frac{
        \sigma_{u_i}\sigma_{u_{i-1}}
        \sqrt{\sigma_1^2-\sigma_{u_{i-1}}^2}
    }{
        \sqrt{\sigma_1^2-\sigma_{u_i}^2}
    }
\right)
\mY .
\end{aligned}
\]
for an increasing sequence of $u_i$. The denoiser is evaluated at the current state depth $u_i$. The formula is singular at the endpoint $u_i=1$, so an implementation needs an endpoint special case or a limiting update.

An alternative to the SB-ODE proposed in this thesis is \textit{direct data prediction}. In \chapref{ch:gradtts} the data prediction objective is used to make the estimator well conditioned to clean data across all noise levels. An added benefit of the data prediction loss is that the estimator becomes a data predictor, and may be a sufficiently strong model without the need to solve an ODE. Here the term ``direct data prediction'' is used to describe the use of a noise conditional model as a data predictor instead of an ODE.

Direct data prediction with data prediction loss is
\[
\mX_0=\mD_\vtheta(\mY, \mY,1)
\]
and for flow-matching loss
\[
\mX_0=\mF_\vtheta(\mY, \mY,1) + \mY
\]
Direct data prediction is distinctly different from solving the SB-ODE because the index passed to the estimator is $u_{i-1}$ for the SB-ODE but $u_{i}$ for DDP. The motivation behind this is that during training the estimator will never see a sample from the noise distribution $\rmX_1$ with index $u=0$. Such an input to the model may act as an adversarial example that the model has not generalised to. Some software include settings for whether the estimator receives $u_i$ or $u_{i-1}$, but there is not much discussion around this.

\section{Example: noise suppression}
Noisy speech is defined as a clean signal $\rmX$ convolved with a room impulse response $\mH$ and additive noise $\rmZ$
\[
\rmY \coloneqq \rmX\ast\mH + \rmZ
\]
The goal of noise suppression is to retrieve $\rmX$ given $\rmY$.

\[
q_1(\rmY)=\int p_z(\rmY - \rmX\ast\mH) q_0(\dd\rmX)
\]

The distributional nature of $\rmX$ and $\rmY$ motivate a general model 
Instead of a variance preserving SDE, consider a variance exploding SDE
\[
\dd\rmX\coloneqq\gamma(\rmY - \rmX_u)\dd u + \sqrt{ck^{2u}}\dd\rmW_t.
\]
\[
\rmM(\mY,\rmX,u;\gamma) = e^{-\gamma u}\rmX + (1- e^{-\gamma u})\mY \qquad \tSigma(u;\gamma,c,k) = \frac{c(k^{2u}-e^{-2\gamma u})}{2(\gamma + \log k)}\tI
\]

When $\mH=I$, the flow-matching target also equals the additive-noise residual. With reverberation, this residual is $\rmY-\rmX=\rmX\ast\mH-\rmX+\rmZ$, so it is not the background noise alone:
\[
\rmZ = \rmY - \rmX
\]
