This chapter investigates conditional likelihood in pretrained image and speech generators. The models considered are Stable Diffusion XL (SDXL) for text-to-image generation, and Grad-TTS and Matcha-TTS for speech synthesis. Together they cover diffusion and conditional flow-matching constructions with different probability paths and conditioning mechanisms. The models are described in Section~\ref{sec:likelihood-models}; the full SDXL evaluation grid is provided in Appendix~\ref{apdx:likelihood-results}.

Following the likelihood derivation for \acp{CNF} in Section~\ref{sec:likelihood-evaluation} \citep{NEURIPS2018_69386f6b}, there have been many developments in forms of \ac{CNF} without measuring if the density behaves as one would expect. For example, the probability-flow \ac{ODE} used with SDXL has a guidance-scale parameter, an extension that introduces a gap in the known properties of its density. Does the evaluated density shift towards images depicting the caption as that scale increases? Grad-TTS also uses an utterance-dependent probability flow conditioned on text $\rvy$. How much does the model rely on the encoded mean $\rmY$, and how much does its likelihood distinguish linguistic content? Matcha-TTS extends these questions to a conditional flow-matching model, examining whether the speech-likelihood behaviour also occurs with a different source distribution and decoder objective.

The experiments address three questions: how the final-data density $p_0(\rmX_u;\vtheta)$ changes along generation, how those changes relate to sample quality, and whether likelihood distinguishes compatible conditioning inputs. SDXL is evaluated under different guidance scales and candidate prompts. Grad-TTS and Matcha-TTS are evaluated through generation trajectories and transcript rescoring, with a further domain-adaptation experiment using Grad-TTS.

The speech models exhibit different density trajectories: Grad-TTS likelihood changes most near the end of generation, whereas Matcha-TTS \ac{BPD} decreases approximately linearly across the evaluated depths. Their recognition trends also differ: trajectory \ac{WER} increases for Grad-TTS but decreases for Matcha-TTS. SDXL likelihood is not monotone along generation, while higher guidance scales are associated with higher likelihood in the reported comparison.

The compatibility evaluations distinguish generation from condition selection. The image prompt-ranking experiment examines whether likelihood selects a caption compatible with an observed image. For both speech generators, likelihood-based transcript rescoring gives higher \ac{WER} than the original \ac{ASR} scores. Grad-TTS rescoring is not significantly better than uniform random candidate selection at the 5\% level for either evaluated \ac{ASR} system; the Matcha-TTS comparisons are reported separately in Section~\ref{sec:likelihood-rescoring}. 

The chapter first describes the models, datasets, and evaluation metrics, then presents the experiments and their results. The final sections summarise the findings and outline future extensions.

\section{Models}
\label{sec:likelihood-models}

SDXL is a latent diffusion model that operates in the latent space of an image autoencoder \citep{podellSDXLImprovingLatent2023}. Generation uses \ac{CFG}. The score network is a U-Net with cross-attention to CLIP text representations \citep{radfordLearningTransferableVisual2021}. The density also operates on the autoencoder representation, not the true image space. The implementation loads the huggingface checkpoint\footnote{\texttt{stabilityai/stable-diffusion-xl-base-1.0}} without the optional refiner. SDXL is trained on an internal web-scale dataset.

Grad-TTS uses a transformer encoder and U-Net diffusion decoder \citep{popov21gradtts}. The encoder predicts a coarse mel-spectrogram which is used as the mean $\rmY$. Although the mel-spectrogram has 80 dimensions, the input dimension of the U-Net is 160 to accommodate the encoder output for conditioning. Grad-TTS is trained on LJSpeech \citep{LJSpeechDataset}.

Matcha-TTS uses a similar yet improved encoder--decoder architecture with an \ac{ODE} decoder optimised by conditional flow matching \citep{mehta2024matcha}. Its source is a standard normal, while the text-encoder output conditions the decoder. Grad-TTS instead uses the text-derived mean in its Gaussian source. Both constructions admit density evaluation through the \ac{ODE} change-of-variables formula. Their different architectures, source laws, and feature normalisations mean that the experiments compare the behaviour of two complete systems rather than isolating the effect of a single probability-path choice.

\paragraph{Image}
Two datasets considered are PACS \citep{liDeeperBroaderArtier2017}, a domain-generalisation dataset with photo, art-painting, cartoon, and sketch domains over seven classes (Figure~\ref{fig:likelihoods-1}),\footnote{The seven PACS categories are dog, elephant, giraffe, guitar, horse, house, and person.} and CLEVR \citep{johnsonCLEVRDiagnosticDataset2017}, a diagnostic dataset of synthetic 3D objects in eight colours. The images for CLEVR are generated following \cite{lewisDoesCLIPBind2023}. A single-object subset of CLEVR is used (Figure~\ref{fig:likelihoods-2}). Images are resized to $512^2$ or $1024^2$. For both datasets, every image type is sampled 20 times.

\paragraph{Speech}
For speech, this work uses the validation sets of LJSpeech \citep{LJSpeechDataset} and TED-LIUM \citep{rousseauTEDLIUMAutomaticSpeech2012}.\footnote{During the writing of this dissertation, TED-LIUM has been removed from official sources due to copyright and licensing issues.} The LJSpeech split contains around 1 hour of public-domain audiobooks read by a US-English female speaker. The TED-LIUM split contains around 3 hours of male speakers and 1 hour of female speaker TED conference audio. The generation-trajectory and transcript-rescoring experiments evaluate both Grad-TTS and Matcha-TTS on LJSpeech. TED-LIUM is used only in the Grad-TTS domain-adaptation experiment.

\section{Evaluation}
Likelihood scores are reported in \ac{BPD}, defined as $-\frac{1}{D}\log_2 p_0(\rmX|\rvy;\vtheta)$, where $\rmX$ is the evaluated representation and $D$ is the reporting normalisation i.e. mel-spectrogram or image dimensionality. Lower \ac{BPD} means higher likelihood. 
Likelihood evaluation uses numerical \ac{ODE} integration with the Dopri5 solver and the Skilling-Hutchinson trace estimator for the divergence. Absolute and relative tolerances default to $10^{-5}$. 

% Source provenance and unresolved run-to-table mapping: LIKELIHOOD_CODE_AUDIT.md.

\paragraph{Image}
For SDXL, each intermediate latent is first decoded to an image and then re-encoded for density evaluation. 
In the SDXL implementation, a reported \ac{CFG} scale of 0 disables the guidance combination but retains positive text conditioning $p_0(\rmX|\rvy;\vtheta)$. It therefore differs from setting the scale to zero in the algebraic guidance formula in Equation~\ref{eq:cfg}, which selects the unconditional score.
The image sample-evaluation grids set both tolerances to $10^{-3}$, while the image prompt-ranking grids evaluate at $10^{-5}$.
\ac{FID} measures a distance between real and synthetic image feature distributions \citep{heusel2017FID}. CLIP cosine similarity measures alignment between text and image representations \citep{radfordLearningTransferableVisual2021}.%\footnote{The specific model used was \texttt{openai/clip-vit-base-patch16}.} 

\paragraph{Speech}
Absolute speech \ac{BPD} values depend on the feature coordinates and normalisation; the trajectory comparisons therefore concern changes within each model.
Grad-TTS evaluates each intermediate spectrogram using the same data-to-source density-integration bounds. Matcha-TTS also evaluates $p_0(\rmX_u|\rvy)$, in its normalised mel-spectrogram coordinates. 
 \Ac{MCD} is a measure of how different two mel-cepstra (audio features) are from each other \citep{kominekSynthesizerVoiceQuality2008}. The \ac{RMSE} of the log fundamental frequencies is denoted as ``log-$F_0$ \ac{RMSE}'' and measures pitch/intonation accuracy. \Ac{MCD} and log-$F_0$ \ac{RMSE} are calculated with the method presented in ESPnet \citep{gaoEUROESPnetUnsupervised2023}. For reference, Tacotron 2 and Fastspeech 2 achieve 0.26 and 0.24 log-$F_0$ \ac{RMSE} respectively \citep{renFastSpeechFastHighQuality2022}.
 
 \Ac{WER} is the ratio of transcription errors to the number of words; calculating the \ac{WER} of the transcript generated by the \ac{ASR} model Whisper medium acts as a proxy for intelligibility \citep{radfordRobustSpeechRecognition2022}. Speaker similarity is measured using \ac{ASV}, by calculating the cosine similarity between embeddings from the speaker verification model Titanet \citep{koluguriTitaNetNeuralModel2021}.

To examine cycle consistency between the source and target distributions, reconstruction is explored. The forward process and then the backward process are applied to a generated image, producing an image similar to the original but with features more consistent with synthetic images (\figref{panda}).\footnote{See \url{https://cocodataset.org/\#explore?id=439326}. In other words, a simple forward-backward-forward iteration can produce ``noise'' with high likelihood. This finding is consistent at $1024\times1024$ resolution, where \ac{BPD} drops from 0.07 to -0.01, with relatively small changes in \ac{FID} and CLIP similarity. \paragraph{Caption ranking} The third and final image experiment evaluates caption ranking. Visual-language reasoning is an important ability for \ac{TTI} models. The results of images depicting textual prompts are impressive, do these capabilities behave similarly when dealing with captions that fit an image? The aim of this experiment is to determine whether diffusion model likelihood can correctly match images to captions from a list of compositionally confounding captions. Although diffusion classifiers show that this is possible with proxy-likelihoods \cite{liYourDiffusionModel2023}, it remains to be determined whether this also holds for exact likelihood. The ability to discern objects is evaluated on CLEVR. For each image, all confounding prompts are presented e.g. the correct prompt is ``a photo of a red sphere'' but instead ``a photo of a red cube'' is provided. The confounding prompt is also used for reconstruction.

Accuracy is calculated based on the number of correct captions that were given the highest likelihood. Both colour and shape are confounded, but not at the same time. Complementary to the CLEVR experiment, PACS is used to measure the likelihood sensitivity to object versus domain changes, for example a photo of a horse.

The results are shown in Table \ref{tab:clevr_pacs}. It can be seen that the scores are unexpectedly low. Scores from random selection and from selecting captions with the highest CLIP score are also provided. The results on CLEVR are comparable to random choice; when reconstruction is used, shape accuracy is reduced. Colour accuracy is improved with 0 \ac{CFG} and reconstruction. This is interesting because reconstruction should steer the image towards the confounding prompt. A possible explanation for this is that the forward process leads to a sparse area of the source distribution, resulting in a lower likelihood image if the caption is incorrect.

SDXL performs better on PACS without reconstruction and guidance. This is anomalous behaviour since the rest of the PACS results are typically 0\% accuracy. This is a surprise given the results in table \ref{tab:sdxl_cfg_reconstruct} show correlation between likelihood and CLIP similarity. This difference may be caused by the introduction of confounding captions. A further possible explanation is that a partial contribution of \ac{CFG} is assigning higher likelihood to all samples, not just those that correctly match a caption.

Table \ref{tab:gtts_over_t} shows that although likelihood increases as $\rmX_1$ is denoised into data $\rmX_0$, the pattern of \ac{BPD} and other metrics is surprising. The \ac{BPD} of the synthesis is -0.08, surpassing the data -0.06, a contrast to the image results which consistently assigned higher likelihood to dataset samples. The largest \ac{BPD} reductions occur in the later stages of the Grad-TTS trajectory. \ac{MCD} decreases and \ac{ASV} increases, while log-$F_0$ \ac{RMSE} changes little. \ac{WER} rises from 8\% at the source to 19\% at the final output, despite the increase in likelihood. Since \ac{WER} is measured with an \ac{ASR} system, this result describes recognition of the generated speech rather than a direct human intelligibility judgement. It nevertheless shows that increasing Grad-TTS density does not ensure a lower transcription error rate.

Matcha-TTS exhibits a different trajectory in Table~\ref{tab:matcha_over_u}. \ac{BPD} decreases from 8.34 to $-1.13$, approximately linearly across the evaluated depths. Over the same trajectory, \ac{MCD} decreases from 13.80 to 5.80, log-$F_0$ \ac{RMSE} decreases from 0.50 to 0.26, and \ac{ASV} increases from $-0.01$ to 0.83. \ac{WER} decreases from 100\% to 4\% and remains at 4\% over the final three reported depths. The source laws provide context for these contrasting starting points: Matcha-TTS begins with zero-mean Gaussian noise, whereas Grad-TTS begins near its text-derived encoder representation. These differences do not by themselves establish a causal effect of source choice, since the models also differ in architecture and optimisation objective.

Both speech models assign their final generated outputs a lower \ac{BPD} than their respective reference recordings. However, only Matcha-TTS shows improving trajectory \ac{WER} alongside increasing likelihood. Transcript rescoring tests a separate property: whether the density of a fixed observed recording ranks alternative texts appropriately.

\paragraph{Nbest list hypothesis rescoring} For each observed LJSpeech recording, an ASR system supplies a list of candidate transcriptions. Each speech generator then evaluates the recording under the alternative texts, and the candidate with highest model likelihood is selected. This evaluates speech--text compatibility directly. The ASR systems are wav2vec2 fine-tuned on 10 minutes of audiobook data \citep{panayotovLibrispeechASRCorpus2015} and Whisper base \citep{radfordRobustSpeechRecognition2022}. Figure~\ref{fig:likelihoods-4} illustrates how Grad-TTS's encoder representation and synthesised features change with the input text. Tables~\ref{tab:asr_reranking} and~\ref{tab:matcha_asr_reranking} report Grad-TTS and Matcha-TTS rescoring, respectively.

For Matcha-TTS, this occurs even though likelihood and recognition accuracy improve together along the generation trajectory. A favourable trajectory trend therefore does not establish that likelihood is effective for selecting among conditioning texts.

\paragraph{Speech domain reconstruction} The final speech experiment examines how Grad-TTS likelihood interacts with intelligibility and speaker similarity under a change of domain. This reconstruction experiment is specific to Grad-TTS; no corresponding Matcha-TTS adaptation result is reported. Grad-TTS is used to adapt TED-LIUM live-talk data \citep{rousseauTEDLIUMAutomaticSpeech2012} to aid an ASR model trained on audiobooks. ASR models perform worse when the acoustic properties differ between train and test data. The spectrogram is passed through a Gaussian blur with kernel 5 and $\sigma=1$, simulating text encoder output $\hat\rmX \approx \rmE_\vphi(\rvy)$ (panel (b) of Figure~\ref{fig:uda-blur-vs-encoder}). The forward ODE is used to calculate $\rmX_1$, and $\rmX_0$ is then produced with the reverse ODE. Given that Grad-TTS is trained on LJSpeech, the newly generated $\rmX_0$ should be acoustically similar to the data the ASR and TTS models were trained on. This is similar to the reconstruction task seen earlier in Table~\ref{tab:sdxl_resolution_reconstruct}. With this in mind, the structure of the mel-spectrogram is largely defined by the text-encoder output, which limits the diffusion modelling capacity to fine detail. This limitation may become apparent for this experiment, and phone duration plays an important role in many ASR systems, and their ability to utilise synthesised speech \cite{nick2023asrtts}.

\section{Summary}
This chapter evaluates conditional likelihood in SDXL, Grad-TTS, and Matcha-TTS through generation trajectories and condition-selection tasks. The results distinguish changes in density from improvements in sample quality and from compatibility with a candidate condition. For SDXL, the reported density, \ac{FID}, CLIP, and prompt-ranking results do not provide interchangeable measures of performance. Their interpretation is restricted to latent-density scoring and the image-evaluation settings described in this chapter.

The speech results depend on the model and the diagnostic. Grad-TTS's largest likelihood increases occur late in generation and coincide with improved speaker similarity but higher trajectory \ac{WER}. Matcha-TTS's likelihood increases more evenly, alongside improvements in spectral, pitch, speaker, and recognition metrics. Nevertheless, likelihood-based transcript selection gives higher \ac{WER} than the original \ac{ASR} scores for both generators. The significance of that degradation differs across conditions: it is significant for both Grad-TTS comparisons and for Matcha-TTS with wav2vec2, but not for Matcha-TTS with Whisper. Thus the rescoring limitation is observed in both diffusion and conditional flow-matching systems, while the trajectory behaviour is not shared by both speech models.

The Grad-TTS domain-adaptation experiment further separates density, speaker similarity, and recognition accuracy: the reconstruction with the highest likelihood also has the highest \ac{WER}. Together, these diagnostics support evaluating conditional models with task-specific measures alongside likelihood. They do not establish that a particular probability path causes the observed behaviour, since the evaluated systems differ in architecture, feature coordinates, conditioning, and numerical implementation.

\section{Future extensions}
Several extensions follow from the evaluations presented here. For SDXL, generation guidance and density-evaluation guidance could be varied independently to distinguish their effects. For speech, the contrasting Grad-TTS and Matcha-TTS trajectories motivate comparing probability paths while controlling the architecture, conditioning inputs, and feature normalisation. Numerical sensitivity could also be assessed by varying solver tolerances and divergence-estimation settings. Since the speech models exhibit different density trajectories, likelihood may be useful in justifying certain scheduled training and sampling schemes e.g. allocate more compute to steps where likelihood changes.

The different relationships between likelihood and intelligibility observed for Grad-TTS and Matcha-TTS motivate evaluating conditional generative models against the requirements of their application. For speech enhancement, the noisy observation already contains linguistic content and acoustic structure that should be preserved in the enhanced output. Paired noisy and clean recordings therefore provide a setting in which to investigate how probability-path design affects restoration quality. Building on the conditioning constructions in Chapter~\ref{ch:simulation-free}, Chapter~\ref{ch:cfmse} compares diffusion, Schr\"odinger-bridge, and flow-matching paths for this task. The investigation examines the role of path variance and added noise, and whether iterative generation is necessary when the source and target recordings are closely related.
