This chapter investigates conditional likelihood in pretrained image and speech generators. The models considered are SDXL for text-to-image generation, and Grad-TTS and Matcha-TTS for speech synthesis. Together they cover diffusion and conditional flow-matching constructions with different probability paths and conditioning mechanisms. The models are described in Section~\ref{sec:likelihood-models}; the full SDXL evaluation grid is provided in Appendix~\ref{apdx:likelihood-results}.

Following the likelihood derivation for \acp{CNF} in Section~\ref{sec:likelihood-evaluation} \citep{NEURIPS2018_69386f6b}, there have been many developments in forms of CNF without measuring if the density behaves as one would expect. For example, the probability-flow ODE used with SDXL has a guidance-scale parameter, an extension that introduces a gap in the known properties of its density. Does the evaluated density shift towards images depicting the caption as that scale increases? Grad-TTS also uses an utterance-dependent probability flow conditioned on text $\rvy$. How much does the model rely on the encoded mean $\rmY$, and how much does its likelihood distinguish linguistic content? Matcha-TTS extends these questions to a conditional flow-matching model, examining whether the speech-likelihood behaviour also occurs with a different source distribution and decoder objective.

The experiments address three questions: how the final-data density $p_0(\rmX_u;\vtheta)$ changes along generation, how those changes relate to sample quality, and whether likelihood distinguishes compatible conditioning inputs. SDXL is evaluated under different guidance scales and candidate prompts. Grad-TTS and Matcha-TTS are evaluated through generation trajectories and transcript rescoring, with a further domain-adaptation experiment using Grad-TTS.

The speech models exhibit different density trajectories: Grad-TTS likelihood changes most near the end of generation, whereas Matcha-TTS BPD decreases approximately linearly across the evaluated depths. Their recognition trends also differ: trajectory WER increases for Grad-TTS but decreases for Matcha-TTS. SDXL likelihood is not monotone along generation, while higher guidance scales are associated with higher likelihood in the reported comparison.

The compatibility evaluations distinguish generation from condition selection. For both speech generators, likelihood-based transcript rescoring gives higher WER than the original ASR scores. Grad-TTS rescoring is not significantly better than uniform random candidate selection at the 5\% level for either evaluated ASR system; the Matcha-TTS comparisons are reported separately in Section~\ref{sec:likelihood-rescoring}. The image prompt-ranking experiment similarly examines whether likelihood selects a caption compatible with an observed image.

The chapter first describes the models, datasets, and evaluation metrics, then presents the experiments and their results. The final sections summarise the findings and outline future extensions.

\section{Models}
\label{sec:likelihood-models}

SDXL is a latent diffusion model that operates in the latent space of an image autoencoder \citep{podellSDXLImprovingLatent2023}. Generation uses classifier-free guidance (CFG). The score network is a U-Net with cross-attention to CLIP text representations. The density also operates on the autoencoder representation, not the true image space. The implementation loads the \texttt{stabilityai/stable-diffusion-xl-base-1.0} checkpoint without the optional refiner. The SDXL report describes pretraining on an internal dataset \citep{podellSDXLImprovingLatent2023}; no specific public training corpus is assumed here.

Grad-TTS uses a transformer encoder and U-Net diffusion decoder \citep{popov21gradtts}. The encoder predicts a coarse mel-spectrogram which is used as the mean $\rmY$. Although the mel-spectrogram has 80 dimensions, the input dimension of the U-Net is 160 to accommodate the encoder output for conditioning. Grad-TTS is trained on LJSpeech \citep{LJSpeechDataset}.

Matcha-TTS uses an encoder--decoder architecture with an ODE decoder optimised by conditional flow matching \citep{mehta2024matcha}. Its source is a zero-mean Gaussian, while the text-encoder output conditions the decoder. Grad-TTS instead uses the text-derived mean in its Gaussian source. Both constructions admit density evaluation through the ODE change-of-variables formula. Their different architectures, source laws, and feature normalisations mean that the experiments compare the behaviour of two complete systems rather than isolating the effect of a single probability-path choice.

\paragraph{Image}
Two datasets considered are PACS \citep{liDeeperBroaderArtier2017}, a domain-generalisation dataset with photo, art-painting, cartoon, and sketch domains over seven classes (Figure~\ref{fig:likelihoods-1}), and CLEVR \citep{johnsonCLEVRDiagnosticDataset2017}, a diagnostic dataset of synthetic 3D objects in eight colours. The images for CLEVR are generated following \cite{lewisDoesCLIPBind2023}. A single-object subset of CLEVR is used (Figure~\ref{fig:likelihoods-2}). Images are resized to $512^2$ or $1024^2$.
The seven PACS categories are dog, elephant, giraffe, guitar, horse, house, and person. These agree with the original benchmark \citep{liDeeperBroaderArtier2017}. The available PACS sample-generation grids request 20 images per class--domain pair, giving a nominal total of $7\times4\times20=560$ images per stage and configuration.
The likelihood of SDXL latents is reported, with \ac{CFG} used to generate PACS samples. Image-distribution distance and text-image similarity are measured using \ac{FID} and CLIP similarity, respectively (Table~\ref{tab:sdxl_over_t}).

\paragraph{Speech}
For speech, this work uses the validation sets of LJSpeech \citep{LJSpeechDataset} and TED-LIUM \citep{rousseauTEDLIUMAutomaticSpeech2012}.\footnote{During the writing of this dissertation, TEDLIUM has been removed from official sources due to copyright and licensing issues.} The LJSpeech split contains around 1 hour of public-domain audiobooks read by a US-English female speaker. The TED-LIUM split contains around 3 hours of male speakers and 1 hour of female speaker TED conference audio. The generation-trajectory and transcript-rescoring experiments evaluate both Grad-TTS and Matcha-TTS on LJSpeech. The Matcha-TTS trajectory evaluation contains 100 recordings; its rescoring conditions each contain 95 recordings and 1560 reference words. TED-LIUM is used only in the Grad-TTS domain-adaptation experiment.

\section{Evaluation}
Likelihood scores are reported in \ac{BPD}, defined as $-\frac{1}{D}\log_2 p_0(\rmX;\vtheta)$, where $\rmX$ is the evaluated representation and $D$ is the reporting normalisation. Lower BPD means higher likelihood. For speech, $D$ is the number of scalar mel-spectrogram values. For SDXL, the reporting scripts use $D=3HW$ for an $H\times W$ RGB image, while the evaluated density is over scaled autoencoder latents. Thus the image scores are latent log-densities normalised by RGB image dimensions, not image-space likelihoods or BPD per latent coordinate.

The SDXL encoder posterior is sampled once for each evaluated image and the resulting latent is multiplied by the autoencoder scaling factor, 0.13025. The autoencoder has four latent channels and downsamples each spatial dimension by eight, giving $4\times64\times64$ latent coordinates for a $512\times512$ image and $4\times128\times128$ for a $1024\times1024$ image. The density is evaluated in these scaled coordinates; no conversion to an unscaled-latent or image-space density is applied. The RGB denominator is 48 times the number of latent coordinates, so these scores should not be compared directly with the speech BPD values.\footnote{Autoencoder configuration for \texttt{stabilityai/stable-diffusion-xl-base-1.0}, revision \texttt{462165984030d82259a11f4367a4eed129e94a7b}: \url{https://huggingface.co/stabilityai/stable-diffusion-xl-base-1.0/blob/462165984030d82259a11f4367a4eed129e94a7b/vae/config.json}.}

Trajectory evaluations apply the final-data density $p_0$ to representations obtained at successive generation depths; they do not evaluate the time-marginal density $p_u$. For SDXL, each intermediate latent is first decoded to an image and then re-encoded for density evaluation. Grad-TTS evaluates each intermediate spectrogram using the same data-to-source density-integration bounds. Matcha-TTS also evaluates $p_0(\rmX_u)$, in its normalised mel-spectrogram coordinates. Its native source-to-data time $s$ is written as $u=1-s$ to retain the chapter convention that generation proceeds from $u=1$ to $u=0$. Absolute speech BPD values depend on the feature coordinates and normalisation; the trajectory comparisons therefore concern changes within each model.

Likelihood evaluation uses numerical ODE integration and a Rademacher trace estimator for the divergence. The available Grad-TTS rescoring scripts use Dopri5 with absolute and relative tolerances of $10^{-5}$. The image sample-evaluation grids set both tolerances to $10^{-3}$, while the image prompt-ranking grids include both $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-5}$.

The image experiment grids assign the same scale to generation or reconstruction and to likelihood evaluation, using values 0, 3, 5, and 7. In the implementation, a reported CFG scale of 0 disables the guidance combination but retains positive text conditioning. It therefore differs from setting the scale to zero in the algebraic guidance formula in Equation~\ref{eq:cfg}, which selects the unconditional score.

\paragraph{Image}

FID measures a distance between real and synthetic image feature distributions \citep{heusel2017FID}. CLIP similarity measures alignment between text and image representations \citep{radfordLearningTransferableVisual2021}. The available implementation uses TorchMetrics' \texttt{FrechetInceptionDistance} and the \texttt{openai/clip-vit-base-patch16} checkpoint for CLIP scoring. Reference images are selected from the PACS manifest for the same class--domain pairs as the generated prompts, converted to RGB, and supplied to the VAE image processor at the requested resolution for density evaluation. FID and CLIP receive PIL-derived image tensors; their input path is separate from VAE preprocessing.

\paragraph{Speech}
 MCD is a measure of how different two mel-cepstra (audio features) are from each other \citep{kominekSynthesizerVoiceQuality2008}. The root mean square error of the log fundamental frequencies is denoted as ``log-$F_0$ RMSE'' and measures pitch/intonation accuracy.\footnote{\Ac{MCD} and log-$F_0$ RMSE are calculated with the method presented in ESPnet \citep{gaoEUROESPnetUnsupervised2023}. For reference, Tacotron 2 and Fastspeech 2 achieve 0.26 and 0.24 log-$F_0$ RMSE respectively \citep{renFastSpeechFastHighQuality2022}.} \Ac{WER} is the ratio of transcription errors to the number of words; calculating the \ac{WER} of the transcript generated by the \ac{ASR} model Whisper medium acts as a proxy for intelligibility \citep{radfordRobustSpeechRecognition2022}. WER is reported as a fraction in the trajectory and adaptation tables, and as a percentage in the rescoring tables. Fractional WER can exceed one because insertions contribute to the error count. The speech-trajectory depth headings are rounded from intervals of one eighth. Speaker similarity is measured using automatic speaker verification (ASV), by calculating the cosine similarity between embeddings from the speaker verification model Titanet \citep{koluguriTitaNetNeuralModel2021}.

To examine cycle consistency between the source and target distributions, reconstruction is explored. The forward process and then the backward process are applied to an image, producing an image similar to the original but with features more consistent with synthetic images (\figref{panda}). Table~\ref{tab:sdxl_resolution_reconstruct} shows that at $u=1$, reconstruction reduces BPD from 0.02 to -0.01 at $512\times512$ resolution. In other words, a simple forward-backward-forward iteration can produce ``noise'' with high likelihood. This finding is consistent at $1024\times1024$ resolution, where BPD drops from 0.07 to -0.01, with relatively small changes in FID and CLIP similarity. At $u=0$, the BPD change is smaller: it is unchanged at the reported precision for $512\times512$ and decreases from -0.08 to -0.09 for $1024\times1024$. FID increases slightly at both resolutions. These results distinguish an increase in likelihood from an improvement in image quality or caption compatibility; reconstruction can also alter the visual appearance, as illustrated by the photograph becoming an illustration in \figref{panda}.

The third and final image experiment evaluates prompt ranking. Visual-language reasoning is an important ability for \ac{TTI} models. The results of images depicting textual prompts are impressive, do these capabilities behave similarly when dealing with captions that fit an image? The aim of this experiment is to determine whether diffusion model likelihood can correctly match images to captions from a list of compositionally confounding captions. Although diffusion classifiers show that this is possible with proxy-likelihoods \cite{liYourDiffusionModel2023}, it remains to be determined whether this also holds for exact likelihood. The ability to discern objects is evaluated on CLEVR. For each image, all confounding prompts are presented e.g. the correct prompt is ``a photo of a red sphere'' but instead ``a photo of a red cube'' is provided. Accuracy is calculated based on the number of correct captions that were given the highest likelihood. Both colour and shape are confounded, but not at the same time. Complementary to the CLEVR experiment, PACS is used to measure the likelihood sensitivity to object versus domain changes, for example a photo of a horse.

Matcha-TTS exhibits a different trajectory in Table~\ref{tab:matcha_over_u}. BPD decreases from 8.34 to $-1.13$, approximately linearly across the evaluated depths. Over the same trajectory, MCD decreases from 13.80 to 5.80, log-$F_0$ RMSE decreases from 0.50 to 0.26, and ASV increases from $-0.01$ to 0.83. WER decreases from 1.00 to 0.04 and remains at 0.04 over the final three reported depths. The source laws provide context for these contrasting starting points: Matcha-TTS begins with zero-mean Gaussian noise, whereas Grad-TTS begins near its text-derived encoder representation. These differences do not by themselves establish a causal effect of source choice, since the models also differ in architecture and optimisation objective.

Both speech models assign their final generated outputs a lower BPD than their respective reference recordings at the reported precision. However, only Matcha-TTS shows improving trajectory WER alongside increasing likelihood. Transcript rescoring tests a separate property: whether the density of a fixed observed recording ranks alternative texts appropriately.

\subsubsection{Transcript rescoring}
\label{sec:likelihood-rescoring}
For each observed LJSpeech recording, an ASR system supplies a list of candidate transcriptions. Each speech generator then evaluates the recording under the alternative texts, and the candidate with highest model likelihood is selected. This evaluates speech--text compatibility directly. The ASR systems are wav2vec2 fine-tuned on 10 minutes of audiobook data \citep{panayotovLibrispeechASRCorpus2015} and Whisper base \citep{radfordRobustSpeechRecognition2022}. Figure~\ref{fig:likelihoods-4} illustrates how Grad-TTS's encoder representation and synthesised features change with the input text. Tables~\ref{tab:asr_reranking} and~\ref{tab:matcha_asr_reranking} report Grad-TTS and Matcha-TTS rescoring, respectively.

\subsubsection{Domain adaptation with Grad-TTS}
\label{sec:likelihood-adaptation}
The final speech experiment examines how Grad-TTS likelihood interacts with intelligibility and speaker similarity under a change of domain. This reconstruction experiment is specific to Grad-TTS; no corresponding Matcha-TTS adaptation result is reported. Grad-TTS is used to adapt TED-LIUM live-talk data \citep{rousseauTEDLIUMAutomaticSpeech2012} to aid an ASR model trained on audiobooks. ASR models perform worse when the acoustic properties differ between train and test data. The spectrogram is passed through a Gaussian blur with kernel 5 and $\sigma=1$, simulating text encoder output $\hat\rmX \approx \rmE_\vphi(\rvy)$ (panel (b) of Figure~\ref{fig:uda-blur-vs-encoder}). The forward ODE is used to calculate $\rmX_1$, and $\rmX_0$ is then produced with the reverse ODE. Given that Grad-TTS is trained on LJSpeech, the newly generated $\rmX_0$ should be acoustically similar to the data the ASR and TTS models were trained on. This is similar to the reconstruction task seen earlier in Table~\ref{tab:sdxl_resolution_reconstruct}. With this in mind, the structure of the mel-spectrogram is largely defined by the text-encoder output, which limits the diffusion modelling capacity to fine detail. This limitation may become apparent for this experiment, and phone duration plays an important role in many ASR systems, and their ability to utilise synthesised speech \cite{nick2023asrtts}.

\section{Summary}
This chapter evaluates conditional likelihood in SDXL, Grad-TTS, and Matcha-TTS through generation trajectories and condition-selection tasks. The results distinguish changes in density from improvements in sample quality and from compatibility with a candidate condition. For SDXL, the reported density, FID, CLIP, and prompt-ranking results do not provide interchangeable measures of performance. Their interpretation is restricted to latent-density scoring and the image-evaluation settings described in this chapter.

The speech results depend on the model and the diagnostic. Grad-TTS's largest likelihood increases occur late in generation and coincide with improved speaker similarity but higher trajectory WER. Matcha-TTS's BPD decreases more evenly, alongside improvements in spectral, pitch, speaker, and recognition metrics. Nevertheless, likelihood-based transcript selection gives higher WER than the original ASR scores for both generators. The significance of that degradation differs across conditions: it is significant for both Grad-TTS comparisons and for Matcha-TTS with wav2vec2, but not for Matcha-TTS with Whisper. Thus the rescoring limitation is observed in both diffusion and conditional flow-matching systems, while the trajectory behaviour is not shared by both speech models.

The Grad-TTS domain-adaptation experiment further separates density, speaker similarity, and recognition accuracy: the reconstruction with the highest likelihood also has the highest WER. Together, these diagnostics support evaluating conditional models with task-specific measures alongside likelihood. They do not establish that a particular probability path causes the observed behaviour, since the evaluated systems differ in architecture, feature coordinates, conditioning, and numerical implementation.

\section{Future extensions}
Several extensions follow from the evaluations presented here. For SDXL, generation guidance and density-evaluation guidance could be varied independently to distinguish their effects. For speech, the contrasting Grad-TTS and Matcha-TTS trajectories motivate comparing probability paths while controlling the architecture, conditioning inputs, and feature normalisation. Numerical sensitivity could also be assessed by varying solver tolerances and divergence-estimation settings. Since the speech models exhibit different density trajectories, likelihood alone does not justify omitting a fixed portion of generation; any change to the numerical generation procedure should be evaluated against speech quality and intelligibility.

The different relationships between likelihood and intelligibility observed for Grad-TTS and Matcha-TTS motivate evaluating conditional generative models against the requirements of their application. For speech enhancement, the noisy observation already contains linguistic content and acoustic structure that should be preserved in the enhanced output. Paired noisy and clean recordings therefore provide a setting in which to investigate how probability-path design affects restoration quality. Building on the conditioning constructions in Chapter~\ref{ch:simulation-free}, Chapter~\ref{ch:cfmse} compares diffusion, Schr\"odinger-bridge, and flow-matching paths for this task. The investigation examines the role of path variance and added noise, and whether iterative generation is necessary when the source and target recordings are closely related.
