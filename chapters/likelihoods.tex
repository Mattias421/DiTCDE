This chapter investigates conditional likelihood in pretrained image and speech generators. The models considered are SDXL for text-to-image generation and Grad-TTS for speech synthesis. These models use different probability paths and conditioning mechanisms. Appendix XXXX provides details on SDXL and Grad-TTS.

Since the derivation of CNF likelihood \citep{NEURIPS2018_69386f6b}, there have been many developments in forms of CNF without measuring if the density behaves as one would expect. For example, the probability-flow ODE used with SDXL has a guidance-scale parameter, an extension that introduces a gap in the known properties of its density. Does the evaluated density shift towards images depicting the caption as that scale increases? Grad-TTS also uses an utterance-dependent probability flow conditioned on text $\rvy$. How much does the model rely on the encoded mean $\rmY$, and how much does it represent linguistic structure?

The above questions are answered through a series of experiments. First the likelihood of a sample through a probability path $p_0(\rmX_u;\vtheta)$ is measured to confirm that likelihood increases during the generative process. The guidance scale is also varied to verify if the density changes. Then, special evaluations are taken to measure expected behaviour for the task. These tasks apply the density model for classification tasks e.g. which transcript fits a spoken utterance the best.

Between the two models, the measured likelihood generally increases as $u\rightarrow 0$, but at different rates: Grad-TTS changes most in the final quarter, whereas SDXL likelihood is not monotone. In the reported CFG comparison, increasing the guidance scale is associated with higher likelihood.

The rest of the evaluations suggest that the densities are not task-orientated. For example, using likelihood for ranking hypothesis transcriptions for speech recognition is not significantly better than random choice. Given a list of confounding prompts, image likelihood struggles to find the correct prompt for an image. Such behaviours are unexpected for a high-quality synthesis model.

The rest of this chapter outlines the models used and data they are tested on. Then details of the evaluation metrics are given. Then the experimental results are presented and discussed. The chapter ends with a final summary paragraph tying everything learned together so far.

\section{Models}

SDXL is a latent diffusion model that operates in the latent space of an image autoencoder \citep{podellSDXLImprovingLatent2023}. Generation uses classifier-free guidance (CFG). The score network is a U-Net with cross-attention to CLIP text representations. The density also operates on the autoencoder representation, not the true image space. SDXL is trained on web-scale data \citep{laion}.

Grad-TTS uses a transformer encoder and U-Net diffusion decoder \citep{popov21gradtts}. The encoder predicts a coarse mel-spectrogram which is used as the mean $\rmY$. Although the mel-spectrogram has 80 dimensions, the input dimension of the U-Net is 160 to accommodate the encoder output for conditioning. Grad-TTS is trained on LJSpeech \citep{LJSpeechDataset}.

\paragraph{Image}
Two datasets considered are PACS \citep{liDeeperBroaderArtier2017}, a domain-generalisation dataset with photo, art-painting, cartoon, and sketch domains over 9 classes (\note{figref}), and CLEVR \citep{johnsonCLEVRDiagnosticDataset2017}, a diagnostic dataset of synthetic 3D objects in eight colours. The images for CLEVR are generated following \cite{lewisDoesCLIPBind2023}. A single-object subset of CLEVR is used (\note{figref}). Each class-domain or shape-colour pair is repeated 20 times. Images are resized to $512^2$ or $1024^2$.
The likelihood of SDXL latents is reported, with \ac{CFG} used to generate PACS samples. Image-distribution distance and text-image similarity are measured using \ac{FID} and CLIP similarity, respectively (Table~\ref{tab:sdxl_over_t}).

\paragraph{Speech}
For speech, this work uses the validation sets of LJSpeech \citep{LJSpeechDataset} and TED-LIUM \citep{rousseauTEDLIUMAutomaticSpeech2012}.\footnote{During the writing of this dissertation, TEDLIUM has been removed from official sources due to copyright and licensing issues.} The LJSpeech split contains around 1 hour of public-domain audiobooks read by a US-English female speaker. The TED-LIUM split contains around 3 hours of male speakers and 1 hour of female speaker TED conference audio.

\section{Evaluation}
Likelihood is reported in \ac{BPD}, defined as $-\frac{1}{D}\log_2 p_0(\rmX;\vtheta)$, where $D=\prod_i d_i$ is the number of scalar dimensions in $\rmX$. Lower BPD means higher likelihood. Density integration uses the adaptive Dormand--Prince fifth-order solver (Dopri5), with absolute and relative tolerances both set to $10^{-5}$. Image likelihood is computed with XXXX CFG. \note{Which cfg scale? 7?}


\paragraph{Image}

FID measures a distance between real and synthetic image feature distributions \citep{heusel2017FID}. CLIP similarity measures alignment between text and image representations \citep{radfordLearningTransferableVisual2021}. 

\paragraph{Speech}
 MCD is a measure of how different two mel-cepstra (audio features) are from each other \citep{kominekSynthesizerVoiceQuality2008}. The root mean squared error of the log fundamental frequencies is denoted as ``log-$F_0$ RMSE'' and measures pitch/intonation accuracy.\footnote{\Ac{MCD} and log-$F_0$ RMSE are calculated with the method presented in ESPnet \citep{gaoEUROESPnetUnsupervised2023}. For reference, Tacotron 2 and Fastspeech 2 achieve 0.26 and 0.24 log-$F_0$ RMSE respectively \citep{renFastSpeechFastHighQuality2022}.} \Ac{WER} is the ratio of transcription errors to the number of words; calculating the \ac{WER} of the transcript generated by the \ac{ASR} model Whisper medium acts as a proxy for ineligibility \citep{radfordRobustSpeechRecognition2022}. Speaker similarity with automatic speaker verification (ASV) is measured by comparing the cosine similarity between embeddings from the speaker verification model Titanet \citep{koluguriTitaNetNeuralModel2021}.

Table~\ref{tab:sdxl_cfg_reconstruct} shows that higher CFG scales are associated with higher likelihood and CLIP similarity for final samples. FID is lowest at CFG scale 5 rather than 7. The full guidance, resolution, reconstruction, and depth combinations are reported in the appendix~\ref{tab:sdxl_over_t_full}.

To examine cycle consistency between the source and target distributions, reconstruction is explored. The forward process and then the backward process are applied to an image, producing an image similar to the original but with features more consistent with synthetic images (\figref{panda}). Table~\ref{tab:sdxl_resolution_reconstruct} shows that at $u=1$, reconstruction reduces BPD from 0.02 to -0.01 at $512\times512$ resolution. In other words, a simple forward-backward-forward iteration can produce ``noise'' with high likelihood. This finding is consistent at $1024\times1024$ resolution, where BPD drops from 0.07 to -0.01, with relatively small changes in FID and CLIP similarity. At $u=0$, the BPD change is smaller: it is unchanged at the reported precision for $512\times512$ and decreases from -0.08 to -0.09 for $1024\times1024$. FID increases slightly at both resolutions. These results distinguish an increase in likelihood from an improvement in image quality or caption compatibility; reconstruction can also alter the visual appearance, as illustrated by the photograph becoming an illustration in \figref{panda}.

We now move to the third and final image experiment. Visual-language reasoning is an important ability for \ac{TTI} models. The results of images depicting textual prompts are impressive, do these capabilities behave similarly when dealing with captions that fit an image? The aim of this experiment is to determine whether DM likelihood can correctly match images to captions from a list of compositionally confounding captions. Although diffusion classifiers show that this is possible with proxy-likelihoods \cite{liYourDiffusionModel2023}, it remains to be determined whether this also holds for exact likelihood. The ability to discern objects is evaluated on CLEVR. For each image, all confounding prompts are presented e.g. the correct prompt is ``a photo of a red sphere'' but instead ``a photo of a red cube'' is provided. Accuracy is calculated based on the number of correct captions that were given the highest likelihood. Both colour and shape are confounded, but not at the same time. Complementary to the CLEVR experiment, PACS is used to measure the likelihood sensitivity to object versus domain changes, for example a photo of a horse.

Table \ref{tab:gtts_over_t} shows that although likelihood increases as $\rmX_1$ is denoised into data $\rmX_0$, the pattern of BPD and other metrics is surprising. The BPD of the synthesis is -0.08, surpassing the data -0.06, a contrast to the image results which consistently assigned higher likelihood to dataset samples. There are many variables that could explain the difference between SDXL and Grad-TTS such as data scale, modality, probability-flow construction. 

This data-level likelihood is only approached after $u\geq0.38$. The other metrics are sensitive to this change in likelihood, with MCD and log-$F_0$ RMSE decreasing and ASV increasing, suggesting that less noisy/corrupt speech that sounds like the target speaker is covered by the model density. WER is also sensitive to likelihood, but unexpectedly increasing. Given the possibility that synthetic data is out of the domain of the Whisper ASR model, it is unknown if speech becomes more unintelligible as likelihood increases. Nevertheless, it is interesting that the source samples have the lowest WER, suggesting that the lexical composition between the source text and synthesised utterance is modelled by the encoder $\rmE_\vphi(\cdot)$, and the diffusion decoder does not model the exact likelihood between text and speech $p_0(\rmX | \rvy)$.

This is verified through the next experiment: \ac{ASR} decoder rescoring on LJSpeech. An ASR pipeline generates a list of hypothesis transcripts for a given utterance, which are scored by a weighted combination of an \ac{AM} probability and a language model probability to find the best transcription. This pipeline serves as a diagnostic tool for how well Grad-TTS likelihoods can measure speech/text compatibility. An example of how the encoder representation and predicted acoustic feature change with input text is shown in \figref{}. The \ac{AM} probabilities are replaced with Grad-TTS likelihoods, and standard hypothesis rescoring techniques are applied \citep{kahnFlashlightEnablingInnovation2022}. The models tested are a wav2vec2 model fine-tuned on 10 minutes of audiobook data \citep{panayotovLibrispeechASRCorpus2015}, and Whisper base \citep{radfordRobustSpeechRecognition2022} (Table~\ref{tab:asr_reranking}).

The final experiment of this chapter questions how DM likelihood interacts with intelligibility and speaker quality. Grad-TTS is used to adapt TED-LIUM live-talk data \citep{rousseauTEDLIUMAutomaticSpeech2012} to aid an ASR model trained on audiobooks. ASR models perform worse when the acoustic properties differ between train and test data. The spectrogram is passed through a Gaussian blur with kernel 5 and $\sigma=1$, simulating text encoder output $\hat\rmX \approx \rmE_\vphi(\rvy)$ (panel (b) of Figure~\ref{fig:uda-blur-vs-encoder}). The forward ODE is used to calculate $\rmX_1$, and $\rmX_0$ is then produced with the reverse ODE. Given that Grad-TTS is trained on LJSpeech, the newly generated $\rmX_0$ should be acoustically similar to the data the ASR and TTS models were trained on. This is similar to the reconstruction task seen earlier \note{table ref}. With this in mind, the structure of the mel-spectrogram is largely defined by the text-encoder output, which limits the diffusion modelling capacity to fine detail. This limitation may become apparent for this experiment, and phone duration plays an important role in many ASR systems, and their ability to utilise synthesised speech \cite{nick2023asrtts}.

\section{Summary}
It is important to understand the nuances of diffusion model likelihood. This is especially true for conditional tasks where
the conditional likelihood is often modelled implicitly, such as with a MR-VP SDE, or
with CFG. The results show that even reasonable assumptions should be verified, as the task and
conditional ODE used have a substantial impact on the nature of DM likelihood. For SDXL, CFG does appear to
model prompt-faithful image generation, but exact likelihood from SDXL cannot be used for
caption classification. This is inconsistent with the concept of Diffusion Classifiers that use
lower bounds on likelihood for classification. For Grad-TTS, the MR-VP SDE likelihood models speaker characteristics and smooth spectrograms,
but not features of language. Such findings are contrary to what one would expect for a
probabilistic conditional process between speech and text.  Hence, these quantitative results highlight a current lack
of understanding of DM likelihood. This chapter provides initial evidence that more
attention should be devoted to experiments and theoretical understanding of DM likelihood,
especially on conditional tasks.

\section{Future extensions}
There are many ways to build off the work presented here. For example, CFG has only lightly been investigated, there are many combinations of using CFG for likelihood computation and CFG for generation, and how the generated samples align with the CFG model density. One finding was that for both models the likelihood only increased in the second half of generation, one possible view of this is that the first half does not contribute to generation. Perhaps likelihood can motivate certain adaptive solvers e.g. skipping the first half of computation. Another extension involves updating the experiments here with more models, and newer models as developments evolve.

Keeping up with new models has been partially covered in \note{appx ref}. Here Matcha-TTS, a flow-matching model, is evaluated instead of Grad-TTS. The result is similar to Grad-TTS, but likelihood progresses linearly rather than slowly at $u<0.5$. As expected, a standard normal has a much worse WER than the Grad-TTS source.
