This chapter investigates the properties of diffusion-model likelihood in practical implementations. The models considered are SDXL for text-to-image generation and Grad-TTS for speech synthesis. These models use different probability paths and conditioning mechanisms. Appendix XXXX provides details on SDXL and Grad-TTS.

Differences between the original model formulations on small-scale data and practical implementations on larger data may affect likelihood. For example, the probability-flow ODE used with SDXL contains a guidance-scale parameter, an extension that introduces a gap in the known properties of its density. Does the evaluated density shift towards images depicting the caption as that scale increases? Grad-TTS also uses an utterance-dependent probability flow conditioned on text $\rvy$. How much does the model rely on the encoded mean $\rmY$, and how much does it represent linguistic structure?

The above questions are answered through a series of experiments. First the likelihood of a sample through a probability path is measured to confirm that likelihood increases during the generative process. The guidance scale is also varied to verify if the density changes. Then, special evaluations are taken to measure expected behaviour for the task. These tasks apply the density model for classification tasks e.g. which transcript fits a spoken utterance the best.

Between the two models, the measured likelihood generally increases as $u\rightarrow 0$, but at different rates: Grad-TTS changes most in the final quarter, whereas SDXL likelihood is not monotone. In the reported CFG comparison, increasing the guidance scale is associated with higher likelihood.

The rest of the evaluations suggest that the densities are not task-orientated. For example, using likelihood for ranking hypothesis transcriptions for speech recognition does not find the correct transcription. Given a list of confounding prompts, image likelihood struggles to find the correct prompt for an image. Such behaviours are unexpected for a high-quality density model.

The rest of this chapter outlines the models used and data they are tested on. Then details of the evaluation metrics are given. Then the experimental results are presented and discussed. The chapter ends with a final summary paragraph tying everything learned together so far.

\section{Models}

SDXL is a latent diffusion model that operates in the latent space of an image autoencoder \citep{podellSDXLImprovingLatent2023}. Generation uses classifier-free guidance (CFG). The score network is a U-Net with cross-attention to CLIP text representations. The density also operates on the autoencoder representation, not the true image space. SDXL is trained on web-scale data \citep{laion}.

Grad-TTS uses a transformer encoder and U-Net diffusion decoder \citep{popov21gradtts}. The encoder predicts a coarse mel-spectrogram which is used as the mean $\rmY$. Although the mel-spectrogram has 80 dimensions, the input dimension of the U-Net is 160 to accommodate the encoder output for conditioning. Grad-TTS is trained on LJSpeech \citep{LJSpeechDataset}.

\paragraph{Image}
Two datasets considered are PACS \citep{liDeeperBroaderArtier2017}, a domain-generalisation dataset with photo, art-painting, cartoon, and sketch domains over 9 classes, and CLEVR \citep{johnsonCLEVRDiagnosticDataset2017}, a diagnostic dataset of synthetic 3D objects in eight colours. The images are generated following \cite{lewisDoesCLIPBind2023}, including a ``red sphere'' example. A single-object subset of CLEVR is used. Each class-domain or shape-colour pair is repeated 20 times. Images are resized to $512^2$ or $1024^2$.
The likelihood of SDXL latents is reported, with \ac{CFG} used to generate PACS samples. Image-distribution distance and text-image similarity are measured using \ac{FID} and CLIP similarity, respectively (Table~\ref{tab:sdxl_over_t}).

\paragraph{Speech}
For speech this work uses the validation sets of LJSpeech \citep{LJSpeechDataset} and TED-LIUM \citep{rousseauTEDLIUMAutomaticSpeech2012}. The LJSpeech split contains around 1 hour of audiobooks read by a US-English female speaker. The TED-LIUM split contains around 3 hours of male speakers and 1 hour of female speaker TED conference audio.

\section{Evaluation}
Likelihood is measured in Bits per dimension (BPD) is $-\frac{1}{D}\log_2 p_0(\rmX;\vtheta)$, where $D=\prod_i d_i$ is the number of scalar dimensions in $\rmX$. Lower BPD means higher likelihood. Likelihood is computed with the RK45 adaptive solver in all cases \note{(verify)}.
\ainote{The original study, \href{https://arxiv.org/html/2409.06364v2}{Section 3}, names dopri5/RK45 for density integration, but this does not verify every run. Check the saved solver configurations, tolerances, and trace-estimator settings. Distinguish density integration from the Euler/RK45 generation comparisons in Table~\ref{tab:uda}; avoid claiming one solver was used for every operation.}


\paragraph{Image}

FID measures a distance between real and synthetic image feature distributions \citep{heusel2017FID}. CLIP similarity measures alignment between text and image representations \citep{radfordLearningTransferableVisual2021}. 

\paragraph{Speech}
\Ac{MCD} is a measure of how different two mel-cepstra (audio features) are from each other \citep{kominekSynthesizerVoiceQuality2008}.\footnote{MCD is calculated with the method presented in ESPnet \citep{gaoEUROESPnetUnsupervised2023}} The root mean squared error of the log fundamental frequencies is denoted as ``log-$F_0$ RMSE'' and measures pitch/intonation accuracy.\footnote{For reference, Tacotron 2 and Fastspeech 2 achieve 0.26 and 0.24 log-$F_0$ RMSE respectively \citep{renFastSpeechFastHighQuality2022}.} \Ac{WER} is the ratio of transcription errors to the number of words; intelligibility is measured by calculating the \ac{WER} of the transcript generated by the \ac{ASR} model Whisper \citep{radfordRobustSpeechRecognition2022}. Speaker similarity with automatic speaker verification (ASV) is measured by comparing the cosine similarity between embeddings from the speaker verification model Titanet \citep{koluguriTitaNetNeuralModel2021}.

To measure how the generative process is intertwined with likelihood, the forward process and then the backward process are applied to an image, producing an image similar to the original but with features more consistent with synthetic images. This is especially problematic in cases where the image changes without reasonable explanation e.g. a photo becoming a cartoon.

Visual-language reasoning is an important ability for \ac{TTI} models. The aim of the final image experiment is to determine whether DM likelihood can correctly match images to captions from a list of compositionally confounding captions. Although diffusion classifiers show that this is possible with proxy-likelihoods \cite{liYourDiffusionModel2023}, it remains to be determined whether this also holds for exact likelihood. The ability to discern objects is evaluated on CLEVR. For each image, all confounding prompts are presented e.g. the correct prompt is ``a photo of a red sphere'' but instead ``a photo of a red cube'' is provided. Accuracy is calculated based on the number of correct captions that were given the highest likelihood. Both colour and shape are confounded, but not at the same time. Complementary to the CLEVR experiment, PACS is used to measure the likelihood sensitivity to object versus domain changes, for example a photo of a horse.

Table \ref{tab:gtts_over_t} shows that although likelihood increases as $\rmX_1$ is denoised into data $\rmX_0$, the effect on other metrics is surprising.
Likelihood is correlated with ASV, marginally with MCD, but not log-$F_0$ RMSE. WER increases as likelihood improves. The results suggest that composition between the source text and hypothesis utterance is modelled by the encoder $\rmE_\vphi(\cdot)$, and the diffusion decoder does not model the exact likelihood between text and speech $p_0(\rmX | \rvy)$.

This is verified through an \ac{ASR} decoder rescoring experiment on LJSpeech. An ASR pipeline generates a list of hypothesis transcripts for a given utterance, which are scored by a weighted combination of an \ac{AM} probability and a language model probability to find the best transcription. This pipeline serves as a diagnostic tool for how well Grad-TTS likelihoods can measure speech/text compatibility. The \ac{AM} probabilities are replaced with Grad-TTS likelihoods, and standard hypothesis rescoring techniques are applied \citep{kahnFlashlightEnablingInnovation2022}. The models tested are a wav2vec2 model fine-tuned on 10 minutes of audiobook data \citep{panayotovLibrispeechASRCorpus2015}, and Whisper \note{small?} (Table~\ref{tab:asr_reranking}).

In all cases, the DM likelihoods yield a worse score than the \ac{AM}, although better than the oracle worst and better than random in the wav2vec2 case. These results are statistically significant using the matched-pair sentence segment word error test \cite{gillickandcox}. This suggests that the likelihoods may have some sensitivity to textual changes but lack an expressive linguistic representation.

To survey how DM likelihood interacts with intelligibility and speaker quality, Grad-TTS is used to adapt TED-LIUM live-talk data \citep{rousseauTEDLIUMAutomaticSpeech2012} to aid an ASR model trained on audiobooks. ASR models perform worse when the acoustic properties differ between train and test data. The spectrogram is passed through a Gaussian blur with kernel 5 and $\sigma=1$, simulating text encoder output $\hat\rmX \approx \rmE_\vphi(\rvy)$. \note{gaus blur plot} The forward ODE is used to calculate $\rmX_1$, and $\rmX_0$ is then produced with the reverse ODE. Given that Grad-TTS is trained on LJSpeech, the newly generated $\rmX_0$ should be acoustically similar to the data the ASR model was trained on. With this in mind, the structure of the mel-spectrogram is largely defined by the text-encoder output, which limits the diffusion modelling capacity to fine detail. This limitation may become apparent for this experiment, and phone duration plays an important role in many ASR systems, and their ability to utilise synthesised speech \cite{nick2023asrtts}.

If Grad-TTS can adapt acoustic-linguistic features, it will reduce WER. Both Euler and RK45 solvers are evaluated (Table~\ref{tab:uda}).

\section{Summary}
It is important to understand the nuances of diffusion model likelihood. This is especially true for conditional tasks where
the conditional likelihood is often modelled implicitly, such as with a MR-VP SDE, or
with CFG. The results show that even reasonable assumptions should be verified, as the task and
conditional ODE used have a substantial impact on the nature of DM likelihood. For SDXL, CFG does appear to
model prompt-faithful image generation, but exact likelihood from SDXL cannot be used for
prompt classification. This is inconsistent with the concept of Diffusion Classifiers that use
lower bounds on likelihood for classification. For Grad-TTS, the MR-VP SDE likelihood models speaker characteristics and smooth spectrograms,
but not features of language. Such findings are contrary to what one would expect for a
probabilistic conditional process between speech and text.  Hence, these quantitative results highlight a current lack
of understanding of DM likelihood. This chapter provides initial evidence that more
attention should be devoted to experiments and theoretical understanding of DM likelihood,
especially on conditional tasks.

$p=3.58\times10^{-24}$). For Whisper, the increase from $3.33\%$ to $4.17\%$
is not significant at the $5\%$ level ($p=0.0818$). Whisper rescoring is also
not significantly better than uniform random selection (exact one-sided
$p=0.395$). Each rescoring condition contains 95 recordings and 1560 reference
words; the trajectory experiment contains 100 recordings. Trajectory WER is a
fraction and can exceed one because of insertions. Here $u=1-s$, where $s$ is
Matcha's source-to-data time. Rounded depth headings represent eighth intervals. Whisper medium for WER and base for nbest list.
